Key Figures in Pan-African & Transnational Black Feminism

Addie Hunton was among the earliest African American women to engage directly in transnational political organizing. As a leader in international women’s and civil-rights organizations, she worked with entities such as the NAACP and the YWCA, and during World War I traveled to Europe to support Black soldiers and observe the global dimensions of racial oppression. Her transnational activism extended into interwar international peace work, and through her writings and organizing she linked racism in the United States with colonial oppression abroad, helping shape an early Black internationalist feminist consciousness.1

Hunton’s involvement in international women’s organizations demonstrated that Black women demanded a seat at global political tables, arguing that racial justice and gender justice must go hand in hand.2 Hunton’s core argument was that the struggle for democracy in America was inseparable from the global struggle against imperialism. She provided evidence for this by documenting the systemic neglect and racism faced by Black soldiers in France, arguing that "the colored soldier" was a symbol of a broader, global color line. This relates to transnational Black feminism by establishing that Black women’s liberation cannot be achieved in isolation; it requires a dismantling of the global structures of white supremacy that connect the American South to the battlefields of Europe and the colonies in Africa.

Amy Ashwood Garvey co-founded the Universal Negro Improvement Association (UNIA) and played a central role in building Pan-African and diaspora networks across the Caribbean, the United States, and Europe. Through her leadership, she helped develop UNIA’s women’s wing and advocated for women’s rights within the broader anti-colonial, Black nationalist movement.3 In 1945, she helped organize the 5th Pan-African Congress in Manchester, where she emphasized that women’s equality was essential to African liberation, planting early seeds for transnational Black feminist politics. Her activism, rooted in both gender and racial justice, shaped a Pan-African vision inclusive of women’s liberation.

“Pan-African feminist histories remind us that Black women have always organized across borders, imagining freedom beyond empire.”

— Dr. Annette Joseph-Gabriel

Garvey’s central argument was that Pan-Africanism would remain incomplete and ineffective if it continued to marginalize women’s labor and leadership. As evidence, she pointed to the 1945 Manchester Congress itself, where she famously critiqued the male-dominated leadership for ignoring the economic and social plight of Black women. This is a foundational tenet of transnational Black feminism: the insistence that racial liberation must include a critique of internal patriarchy, ensuring that "freedom" applies to the entire diaspora regardless of gender.

As an anthropologist, journalist, and global activist, Eslanda Robeson travelled extensively across Africa in the 1930s and 1940s. Her book African Journey (1945) documented anti-colonial movements and drew attention to the political roles of African women, bridging African American experiences of racial oppression with African struggles for liberation.4 Robeson’s transnational activism and scholarship combined anti-racism, anti-colonialism, and feminist critique. Her work made clear that the fight for justice in the United States was intimately linked with liberation for colonized peoples abroad.

Robeson argued against the Western anthropological view of African women as "passive" or "primitive." Her evidence was rooted in her field observations of African women’s sophisticated social and political networks, which she presented as a model for global resistance. By positioning African women as political protagonists, she linked the domestic "Civil Rights" struggle to a global "Human Rights" struggle. This furthers the idea of transnational Black feminism by creating an intellectual bridge that allows Black women in the West to see their liberation as technically and politically tied to the sovereignty of African nations.

Una Marson was a Jamaican poet, playwright, journalist, and social activist who became an internationally recognized feminist and Pan-Africanist in the 1930s. Between 1932 and 1936, she lived in London and became active in British and international women’s and Pan-African organizations. Marson’s experiences of racism and sexism in Britain shaped her evolving vision of Black feminism and internationalism.5 Her writing, activism, and engagement with global Black intellectual networks make her a foundational, though long overlooked, figure in transnational Black feminist history.

“Pan-African feminist traditions grew from African women’s own political, social, and intellectual frameworks—not from Western feminist models.”

— Ifi Amadiume

Marson’s primary argument was that Black women faced a "double jeopardy" of racial and gendered exclusion that neither white feminist nor Black male-led organizations fully addressed. She used her creative work and journalism as evidence, highlighting the psychological toll of colonial beauty standards and the economic marginalization of Caribbean women. Her work is a cornerstone of transnational Black feminism because it articulates a specific "intersectional" identity that crosses borders—showing how the Black female body becomes a site of political struggle in both the colony (Jamaica) and the metropole (London).

Though often more recognized for her national civil-rights and suffrage work, Mary Church Terrell played a significant role in early international feminist and Black women’s activism. For instance, she was involved in the founding of the International Council of Women of the Darker Races (ICWDR), which sought to link women across the African diaspora, making her efforts an early form of transnational organizing among Black women.6 Her life and activism show that from as early as the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, Black American women were thinking and acting internationally.

Terrell’s argument was that elite and educated Black women had a "transnational responsibility" to foster solidarity with women of color globally to challenge the "global color line." The evidence for this was her work with the ICWDR, which established study circles to learn about the history and conditions of women in Haiti, Africa, and Asia. This relates to transnational Black feminism by demonstrating that early organizers did not just focus on local suffrage, but sought to create a global "counter-knowledge" that challenged the Eurocentric narratives of the time.

As a pioneering African feminist scholar, Ifi Amadiume challenged Western assumptions about gender and gender relations. Through landmark works such as Male Daughters, Female Husbands, she documented flexible, pre-colonial gender systems in Igbo society, providing theoretical grounding for understanding African women’s agency beyond colonial and patriarchal frameworks.7 Although her writing began later (from the 1980s onward), Amadiume’s scholarship has been integral in connecting African feminist theory to global Black feminist debates, demonstrating the long intellectual lineage and continuous evolution of transnational feminist critique.

Amadiume’s core argument is that "gender" is a social construct that was practiced with significant flexibility in pre-colonial Africa, and that European colonialism forcibly imposed a rigid, biological patriarchy on these societies. She uses the Igbo concepts of "Male Daughters" (where women could inherit male status to maintain lineage) and "Female Husbands" (where women could gain the status of husband through economic achievement) as evidence that power was not always tied to biological sex. This is vital to transnational Black feminism because it decolonizes the concept of "womanhood," proving that African women have their own indigenous histories of power that predate and exist outside of Western feminist frameworks.

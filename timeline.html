Timeline: A Century of Transnational Black Feminist Resistance

This timeline traces the evolution of transnational Black feminist thought, showing how Black women moved from early international organizing to the development of formal academic theory. Across continents and generations, these figures articulated a shared understanding of racial, gendered, and colonial oppression as global and interconnected.

Phase 1: Early International Consciousness (Late 1800s – 1920s)

  • 1890s – 1920s: Mary Church Terrell begins organizing with the International Council of Women. She argues that Black women’s liberation is a global human rights issue, not just a domestic civil rights one.
  • 1917 – 1919: Addie Hunton travels to France during WWI. Her work links the racism faced by Black American soldiers to the colonial oppression of African and Caribbean people, creating the first "transnational" link in your analysis.
  • 1918: Amy Ashwood Garvey co-founds the UNIA in Jamaica. She begins building the foundation for a Pan-Africanism that explicitly includes women’s leadership and economic autonomy.

Phase 2: The Interwar & Anti-Colonial Surge (1930s – 1940s)

  • 1932 – 1936: Una Marson moves to London. Her work with the League of Coloured Peoples identifies the "double jeopardy" of being Black and female in the heart of the British Empire.
  • 1936: Eslanda Robeson begins her extensive travels across the African continent. Her anthropological fieldwork challenges Western myths by documenting the political power of African women.
  • 1945: The 5th Pan-African Congress (Manchester). Amy Ashwood Garvey and other women demand that gender equality be a central pillar of African independence movements.

Phase 3: Connecting Movement to Theory (1980s – Present)

  • 1987: Ifi Amadiume publishes Male Daughters, Female Husbands. Her work provides the "theoretical grounding" for the activism seen in the previous decades, proving that the flexible gender systems these women fought for were rooted in indigenous African history.
  • Modern Day: The "Global Map of Resistance" continues as contemporary Black feminists use these historical frameworks to fight global systems of patriarchy and neo-colonialism.
